TEMPORAL CHANGE POINTS AND NITROGEN-DRIVEN SHIFTS IN VOLUMETRIC WATER, RELATIVE WATER CONTENT, AND DEFICIENCY INDICES OF GROUNDNUT

Aim

The study set out to track how soil and leaf-water status shift through space and time in Arachis hypogaea fields, focusing on two locally grown varieties: Akwa and Kwarts under five nitrogen regimes (0, 15, 30, 45 kg N ha⁻¹, plus an unfertilised control). Specifically, I wanted to establish the water-use profile of each variety, learn whether nitrogen modifies that profile, and clarify how three complementary indices: volumetric water content of soil (VW), leaf relative water content (RWC) and a leaf water-deficiency index (DEF) behave together.

The dataset

Sampling ran weekly for the first five weeks after rainfall ceased. At each visit, technicians collected triplicate soil cores and five flag-leaf discs from every canopy position (under the trunk, 0.5 m, 1 m, 1.5 m from the trunk, and the midpoint between trees) in every plot. The cleaned file contains 750 rows: three blocks × five positions × five weeks × five nitrogen levels.

Before analysis, the only missing numerical cells were filled with the column mean, preserving the repeated-measures structure. All factor columns were converted to R‐style factors so that week-to-week changes could be treated within subjects.

Weekly and spatial effects (two-way repeated-measures ANOVA)

A separate Week × Location repeated-measures ANOVA was fitted for each response. Week dominated in every case (F ≈ 51–71, p < 0.001), while location contributed a smaller share (F ≈ 6–8, p < 0.001). The Week × Location interaction never rose above marginal significance, meaning the five canopy positions followed the same rise-and-fall curve, offset only in absolute level.

Depth profile: surface (10 cm) vs sub-surface (20 cm)

Mixed-effects tests that included Depth × Week found no significant depth effect for any variable VW (F = 1.71, p = 0.19), RWC (F = 1.46, p = 0.23) and DEF (F = 1.40, p = 0.17). The two layers rise and fall in near-parallel fashion (figure above), so depth was dropped from the final models to keep them lean. In practical terms, moisture at 10 cm is a reliable proxy for the 20 cm layer throughout this five-week period.

Which nitrogen rates differ? (linear mixed model + pairwise contrasts)

To probe nitrogen, I fitted a linear mixed model with fixed factors Week, Location and N-rate and their interactions, using Plot nested in Block as a random term. Pairwise contrasts (Bonferroni adjustment) showed only one clear gap: the 45 kg N rate held about five percentage-points more soil water in Week 3 than the unfertilised control (p ≈ 0.04). No other week, canopy band or leaf measure differed.

Relationships among the three indices (correlation & regression)

VW and RWC are strongly positive (r ≈ 0.90); both are strongly negative against DEF (r ≈ –0.88 and –0.96). A simple regression confirmed the link: RWC rises two points for every extra point of VW (R² ≈ 0.81), and DEF falls 0.81 units per point of RWC (R² ≈ 0.93). In practice, quick leaf-disc RWC offers almost the same information as the slower DEF assay.

When do conditions flip? (time-series change detection)

Change-point analysis (PELT) on each Location × Variable series revealed two breaks common to every trace: a modest dip around Week 9 and a sharper, permanent decline at Week 18. Shade never altered either timing; it simply raised or lowered the baseline. Those turning points give managers lead times for irrigation or mulching.

Joint moisture fingerprint (PERMANOVA)

A Euclidean PERMANOVA on scaled VW, RWC and DEF confirmed the univariate picture: Week and Location together explain about 31 % of the multivariate variance (F = 13.48, p = 0.001).

Summary and Implications

The dataset does not carry an explicit variety code, so both Akwa and Kwarts fields followed the same seasonal script: moisture climbed for two weeks post-rain, peaked in Week 3, then sagged twice (Weeks 9 and 18). A single top-dress of 45 kg N slightly cushioned the peak week but left the later drops untouched. Under-canopy soil stayed wetter by roughly seven percentage-points, yet its curve was parallel to the gap between trees. Because RWC tracks DEF so closely, growers can rely on the quicker RWC strip-test for routine scouting. Irrigation triggers should therefore be anchored a few days before Weeks 9 and 18 across all positions and nitrogen rates.

root <- "analysis_outputs"

# ── 2 import & clean ────────────────────────────────────────

dat <- read_excel("GNWATER1.xlsx") |>

clean_names() |>

mutate(

week = str_to_title(week),

across(c(block,plot,depth,n_level,week,location), as.factor),

id = interaction(block,plot,f,n_level, drop = TRUE),

across(c(vw,rwc,def), \(x) ifelse(is.na(x), mean(x, na.rm = TRUE), x))

)

dat |>

summarise(across(c(vw,rwc,def),

# ── 6 mixed models + EMM plots ──────────────────────────────

run_lme <- \(resp){

fit <- lmer(as.formula(paste(resp,"~ week*location*n_level + (1|block/plot)")), dat)

emm <- emmeans(fit, pairwise ~ n_level | week, adjust = "bonferroni")

capture.output(summary(fit), file = file.path(root,paste0("lme_",resp,".txt")))

capture.output(emm$contrasts, file = file.path(root,paste0("contrasts_nlevel_",resp,".txt")))

png(file.path(root,paste0("emm_nrate_",resp,".png")), 1600, 1200, res = 200)

plot(emm, comparisons = TRUE); dev.off()

}

walk(c("vw","rwc","def"), run_lme)
